The model calls 39.8% of held-out games exactly right against 35.5% for always guessing the most common tier, and sits 0.96 tiers from the truth on average against 0.90 for always guessing the median tier.

It does not clear the bar, so jobs/train_model.py wrote no artifact and /games/{appid}/prediction keeps serving the rule-based baseline, tagged so a reader can tell which produced a number.

01 / What shipped

A pipeline, deliberately not an accuracy claim

Phase 2 was scoped to the infrastructure rather than the result, because the two are separable and only one of them was achievable at 31 labeled rows. The training script, the feature contract, the evaluation protocol, the serving path and the retraining job all exist and are tested. Whether the model is any good is a question about the data, and the data currently answers no.

The model is a Frank & Hall ordinal decomposition over gradient-boosted trees: three binary classifiers — is this above Flop, above Underperform, above Success — recombined into a distribution over the four tiers. The tiers are an ordered scale, so mistaking a breakout for a success is a small error and mistaking it for a flop is a large one. A plain four-way classifier scores both as simply wrong and optimises accordingly.

02 / Discipline

Everything that would have made it look good

The corpus is mostly post-launch data. Launch review volume, launch sentiment, retention, peak concurrents, how the studio fared afterwards — all of it is sitting in the same tables, and all of it would raise measured accuracy while destroying the model's purpose. A forecaster that reads launch sentiment is not forecasting. The failure would be invisible in the accuracy number, which is exactly why it is enforced in code rather than left to discipline.

Found during the build

The publisher aggregate was leaking, twice. The feature matrix originally read a stored publisher_stats table. That aggregate includes the game being predicted — so a title's own launch sentiment reached its own feature vector through its publisher's average, and launch sentiment is most of what the label is derived from.

It also includes releases that came after the game. Predicting a 2019 launch from a publisher's 2024 track record is not a forecast; it is hindsight wearing a feature's clothes.

The record is now recomputed per row with both excluded, which no cached table can express. A publisher's debut correctly gets no history at all rather than an average of its own future — which is why only 26 of 31 rows carry any publisher history at all.

Metacritic is on the forbidden list for a reason worth stating: critic scores land at launch under embargo, not before it. A field being available in the row does not make it available in time.

03 / Protocol

How it was scored, and why not the obvious way

At 31 rows, how the model is measured matters more than how it is trained. A single 25% holdout leaves a test set small enough that each individual game moves the headline accuracy by double digits — the number would describe the fold assignment, not the model. None is reported here.

Instead: repeated stratified k-fold, 5 folds × 20 repeats. Every game is scored only while held out, and every game is held out many times, so the figures average over fold luck rather than inheriting it. Stratified because the rarest tier has few enough members that an unstratified fold can miss it entirely — which is also what caps the fold count at 5.

The bar

A constant guess, not the rule-based baseline. The baseline does not clear the constant either (32.3% against 35.5%), so beating it would prove nothing.

Two constants

The modal tier is the strongest trivial guess for accuracy; the median tier is the strongest for ordinal distance. Each metric faces its own, not one convenient straw man.

Computed inside the fold

Both constants are recomputed from each training split. A constant derived from the full labeled set already knows the answer distribution of the rows it is scored on.

No tuning

Hyperparameters are fixed a priori and never searched. At this sample size a search finds the split that flatters the model, and the reported figure quietly becomes a training score.

04 / Result

Better at being exactly right, worse at being close

The split verdict is the whole finding. The model is ahead on accuracy and behind on ordinal distance: it gets more calls exactly right, and its wrong calls are further from the truth than simply naming the middle tier every time.

GateFailsdistance CI must clear zero

The gate asks for the ordinal-distance improvement to clear its own uncertainty, not merely to be positive. Being ahead on a mean is not evidence when the interval straddles zero, and at this sample size the interval is wide enough to swallow most effects. Here it does not even need to: the improvement is negative before the interval is applied.

Why this is the right outcome

A model that cannot beat a constant guess has learned nothing. Serving it anyway would attach a probability distribution and a confidence label to noise, and the dashboard would present that with the same furniture it uses for real forecasts. Refusing costs a feature; shipping costs the reader's ability to trust any number on the page.

06 / Reading

What this does and does not establish

  • EstablishedThe pipeline works. Features, leakage guard, ordinal model, cross-validation, the gate, serving with fallback, and a retraining job that skips unchanged labels — all built and tested. None of it needs revisiting when the corpus grows.
  • EstablishedStructural pre-launch features do not separate these outcomes at this sample size. Publisher record, price against the cohort's going rate, platform reach, release timing — measured together, they lose to naming the middle tier.
  • Not establishedThat pre-launch forecasting is impossible. 31 rows cannot distinguish a data-volume problem from a feature problem from a genuine ceiling. Nothing here decides that, and any claim that it does is reading past the evidence.
  • Not establishedThat the model would fail with better features. Wishlist rank, marketing spend, franchise history and pre-launch buzz are all absent — not tested and found wanting, simply absent.
07 / Next

What would move this

Labels, mostly. The corpus holds 204 games and 34 labels. Roughly 150 labeled rows is where cross-validation stops being the limiting factor and a held-out test set becomes affordable. That is research time, not engineering time.

Auto-labeling the remaining games with the Phase 1 rubric would be fast and worthless: a model trained on rubric labels learns the rubric, not the outcome. The informative labels are precisely the ones the rubric cannot settle on its own.

The retraining job runs on the 1st and 15th, skips when no new labels have landed, and re-runs this same evaluation each time. If the corpus ever grows enough for the model to clear the bar, an artifact gets written and the endpoint switches over on its own. Nothing needs to be flipped by hand.
