Correction

Launch size measures attention, not success

The first version of the rubric led with cohort-normalized launch volume, following the Phase 0.5 finding that volume separated the labeled extremes. It failed in one direction, consistently: seven of ten underperformers came back as successes.

The reason is visible in the data. Battlefield 2042 opened in the 89th percentile of its release cohort at 34% positive. Starfield: 88th percentile, 66% positive. PAYDAY 3: 74th, 37%. A hyped AAA release draws an enormous launch crowd whether or not it deserves one, and volume alone cannot see the collapse that follows.

Sentiment + floor + retention100%

Both figures are the volume-led and current rubrics scored on the same 204-game corpus, so the comparison isolates the rubric change. On the smaller 126-game corpus the volume-led version scored 74.2% — it got worse as peers were added, because wider percentile spread pushed still more underperformers above the volume floor.

Launch sentiment turned out to be the strongest single separator, with two corrections: a volume floor to catch well-reviewed releases nobody bought, and a retention check for launches that recovered.

Signal

Retention catches what sentiment misses

One release breaks the sentiment rule cleanly. Helldivers 2 opened at 75.2% positive — below the bar, through days of server failures — and is unambiguously a breakout. What separates it from a genuine disappointment is what happened next: it accrued 5.9× its launch-window reviews by the three-month mark, against 1.0–1.7× for every flop in the set.

That ratio is only available because Phase 0.5 stored windowed metrics rather than lifetime totals. It now does two jobs in the rubric: it rescues a high-volume launch that reviewed badly but kept growing, and it promotes a strong release to breakout when its audience compounded (Space Marine 2, Clair Obscur).

Where it agrees and where it doesn’t

The two disagreements

The four-tier figure holds at 93.5%. Both remaining disagreements are worth more than the headline number, because neither is noise.

Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League

label: flop  ·  rubric: underperform  ·  44th-percentile volume, 75% positive

Rocksteady was not closed and no mass layoffs were tied to the title, so the rubric’s post-launch test lands on underperform. The label rests on Warner Bros. Discovery’s confirmed ~$200M write-down — a publisher-level financial signal the rubric has no field for. It was already carried at medium confidence.

S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2: Heart of Chornobyl

label: success  ·  rubric: breakout  ·  92nd-percentile volume, 83% positive

This one is new, and it is the overfitting caveat made concrete. Against the 126-game corpus S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 sat at the 88th percentile and the rubric called it a success. Adding 78 peers moved it to the 92nd, across the 90th-percentile breakout gate, and the call flipped — without a line of the rubric changing.

The threshold was not moved to make this go away, and it should not be: a game one percentile from a boundary will flip whenever the reference set shifts. It is also arguable the rubric is right and the label was conservative — 1M units in 48 hours and a 117,928 concurrent peak is breakout-shaped.

Caveat

What this number is not

Read before quoting 96.8%

The thresholds were fitted after seeing these 31 games. 78% sentiment, a 35th-percentile floor, 2.5× retention — each was chosen by looking at where the labeled set separated. Agreement measured on the same games is in-sample and optimistic, and would not survive contact with 31 new releases unchanged.

Growing the corpus to 204 was a partial test of that: the peer set every percentile is computed against changed substantially, the thresholds stayed put, and the falsifiable axis went up. That is encouraging but not a holdout — the labeled games themselves are unchanged. And S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 flipping tiers on a four-percentile shift shows how little weight any single boundary can carry.

What the exercise establishes is the structure: that sentiment beats volume as the primary separator, that a volume floor is needed to catch well-reviewed titles nobody bought, and that retention carries information neither of them has. Those findings should survive; the specific constants should not be trusted to three significant figures.

Phase 2 should hold out a labeled subset before training, rather than tuning against everything available.

Labels

What got labeled, and what deliberately didn’t

34 of 204 rows carry an outcome, each with cited sources and an explicit confidence level. Every label came from published reporting on studio outcomes, layoffs, sales disclosures and post-launch support — not from recollection. The other 170 carry full API data and await research.

Labels are Steam-scoped, and that excluded good candidates

The spec measures outcome tiers against Steam-specific performance. That rules out delayed ports, whose commercial story happened elsewhere: Titanfall 2’s Steam launch reviews at 96% positive, while the “underperform” outcome everyone knows belongs to its 2016 Origin release. Labeling it would teach a model the exact inverse of the truth.

So Titanfall 2, Days Gone, Saints Row and Monster Hunter: World are researched and carry launch-type metadata, but no label. Three seed rows labeled before this rule — Marvel’s Spider-Man 2, Star Wars Outlaws and Skull and Bones — are flagged in their notes and excluded from scoring.

What Phase 1 adds to the repo

app/rubric.py

The rubric, as code

Observed launch metrics plus post-launch signals to one of four tiers, with the reasoning attached. Returns unresolved rather than guessing when the evidence to separate Flop from Underperform hasn’t arrived — which is exactly the “Failed to Meet Expectations” state.

app/cohort.py

Cohort normalization

Percentile ranks within a rolling three-year window, so counts stay comparable as Steam’s baseline grows. Refuses to return a percentile when the cohort is too small to rank against.

app/baseline.py

Pre-launch baseline

The floor a trained model has to beat: a structural prior from how the same team’s past day-one releases resolved. Served at GET /games/{appid}/prediction, always at low confidence, tagged with the method that produced it.

migration 0003

Structured signals

studio_signal and support_signal as separate axes. A studio can be gutted and still finish the season pass (Callisto Protocol); a healthy studio can walk away from a title (Marvel’s Avengers). Telling Flop from Underperform needs both.

Open

Carried into Phase 2

  • RecoveryResolved: outcomes describe the launch. No Man’s Sky is now underperform on its launch window despite its ten-year recovery. Recovery is real and worth tracking, but as its own signal — folding it into the launch label would train launch-window features to predict something they cannot contain.
  • OverfittingHold out labels before training. The rubric’s constants were tuned on all 31 scored games. Phase 2 should split the set and report out-of-sample performance.
  • Labels170 rows still unlabeled. Full API data, no outcome. Corpus growth already fixed the cohort baselines — every release year now clears the reliability floor — but the base rate and any trained model still need labels, and the research pass is the bottleneck.
  • PricesNominal price isn’t comparable across years. $60 was the going rate through 2022 and $70 from 2023, and $60 in 2015 is worth about $80 in 2025 terms — so the industry’s rise was a real-terms cut. PriceIndex now ranks price against its cohort’s modal rate rather than deflating by CPI, which game prices do not track. It only produces a rate for cohorts with enough curated prices, so today that means 2022 onward.
  • Publisher signalNo field for a publisher-level financial write-down. That is what the Suicide Squad label rests on and the rubric cannot see it.
  • Critic scoresStill only 72 of 126. Unchanged from Phase 0.5; OpenCritic remains the next data source, and critic-vs-user divergence is a Phase 3 signal that needs it.
  • Free-to-playHalo Infinite has no unit sales to measure. It was labeled on engagement and roadmap delivery instead. The rubric has no explicit handling for free-to-play, and more of them will arrive.
